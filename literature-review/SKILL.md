# Literature Review

When a question has been studied before, the fastest route to a reliable answer is to find and synthesise that research rather than relying on a few articles or one memorable study. A literature review gathers relevant studies, assesses their quality and summarises what the evidence shows, where it agrees, where it conflicts and what is still unknown. Systematic reviews in medicine and social science follow formal protocols such as PRISMA for transparent reporting. [Reference, PRISMA statement] Most work and learning needs a lighter but still disciplined version: a rapid evidence review.

This skill runs literature reviews for work, study and policy questions: defining the question and inclusion criteria, searching academic databases, screening studies, extracting key data, assessing quality, synthesising findings and writing a clear summary with confidence levels and gaps.

## Operating principles
1. **Question and criteria first.** Decide what counts as a relevant study before searching.
2. **Search beyond one database.** Combine sources to avoid missing important work.
3. **Prefer reviews and strong designs.** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses summarise many studies.
4. **Judge quality, not just results.** Sample size, design and bias matter.
5. **Synthesise, don't list.** Explain patterns across studies, not each paper in turn.
6. **Be transparent.** Record searches, criteria and decisions so others can check them.

## Review types
| Type | Time | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Quick evidence scan | Hours | Orientation; low-stakes questions |
| Rapid evidence review | Days to two weeks | Business and policy decisions |
| Narrative review | Weeks | Broad topics, theory, teaching |
| Systematic review | Months | Definitive answers; academic and clinical work |

## Where to search
| Source | Strengths |
|---|---|
| Google Scholar | Broad coverage, citations, "cited by" links |
| PubMed | Health and life sciences |
| Semantic Scholar | AI-assisted discovery and summaries |
| SSRN, arXiv, RePEc | Working papers in social science, economics, computing (check peer review status) |
| Cochrane Library | Systematic reviews in health |
| University and think-tank repositories | Reports and evaluations |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when a decision depends on what research shows, for evidence-based policies and practices, academic work, writing authoritative content and checking popular claims about studies.

Don't use it for questions about your own customers or markets that published research cannot answer; use interviews, surveys and data instead.

## Inputs
- The question and the decision or purpose.
- Population, setting, intervention or factor and outcomes of interest.
- Time range and languages.
- Depth and deadline.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Frame the question
Use a structure such as population, intervention or exposure, comparison and outcome: "In remote software teams (population), does pair programming (intervention) compared with solo work (comparison) improve code quality (outcome)?"

### Step 2. Set inclusion criteria
Study types, dates, populations, settings and languages to include or exclude.

### Step 3. Build search strings
Key terms and synonyms combined with AND and OR; test and refine.

### Step 4. Search and record
Run searches in chosen databases; record date, string and number of results.

### Step 5. Screen
Titles and abstracts first, then full texts against criteria; record reasons for exclusion.

### Step 6. Extract data
For each included study: citation, design, sample, setting, measures, main findings, effect size and limitations.

### Step 7. Assess quality
Design strength, sample size, risk of bias, funding and conflicts, replication.

### Step 8. Synthesise
Group findings by theme or outcome; describe consistency, size of effects, conditions where results differ and overall strength of evidence.

### Step 9. Write up
Summary answer, confidence level, key studies, gaps and implications for the decision.

## Search string example
```
("remote work" OR "telework" OR "work from home") AND (productivity OR performance) AND (software OR engineering)
```

## Extraction table
```
| Study | Design | Sample | Setting | Outcome | Finding | Effect size | Quality | Notes |
| Author 2023 | RCT | 1,600 employees | Travel company | Productivity, retention | Hybrid no worse on performance; lower attrition | Small | Medium-high | One company |
```

## Synthesis template
```
Question: <...>
Bottom line: <what the evidence suggests, in two sentences>
Confidence: <high/medium/low and why>
What studies agree on: <...>
Where results differ and why: <settings, methods, populations>
Gaps: <what has not been studied well>
Implications for our decision: <...>
Key sources: <5 to 10 strongest studies>
```

## Finding more studies
- Backward search: check the reference lists of key papers.
- Forward search: use "cited by" to find newer work that built on them.
- Look for recent systematic reviews first; they point to the main studies.
- Search author names of leading researchers in the field.

## Quality questions for each study
| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Was there a comparison group? | Without one, effects are hard to attribute |
| How were participants chosen? | Self-selection can bias results |
| How large was the sample? | Small samples give unstable results |
| Were outcomes measured objectively? | Self-reports can inflate effects |
| Who funded it? | Interests can shape design and reporting |
| Has it been replicated? | Single findings may not hold |

## Using AI tools for reviews
AI tools can suggest search terms, find candidate papers, summarise abstracts and fill extraction tables. Verify that each paper exists, check key numbers against the full text and make screening and quality judgements yourself for included studies. Record which steps used AI.

## Presenting evidence to non-experts
Lead with the bottom line and confidence, explain in plain words what kinds of studies support it, give one or two concrete examples and say what is still uncertain. Avoid listing papers; decision makers need the pattern and its strength, with references available for anyone who wants detail.

Update the review when major new studies appear.

Share the search log with the summary.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Very few studies | Say so; widen criteria carefully or use expert input |
| Many low-quality studies | Weight by quality; do not count papers as votes |
| Conflicting findings | Look for differences in populations, methods and measures |
| Only preprints | Label clearly; check for later peer-reviewed versions |
| Industry studies | Include with caution; note sponsors |
| Popular claim not supported | Explain what research actually shows |

## Worked example
An HR lead needed evidence on whether hybrid work affects productivity before a policy change. A rapid evidence review searched three databases with defined criteria, screened 140 results to 18 studies and extracted design, setting and findings. Synthesis found that well-designed experiments generally showed hybrid work did not reduce measured performance and often improved retention, while results for fully remote work varied by role and measurement. Confidence was labelled medium, gaps were noted for creative and junior roles, and the policy kept hybrid work with support for new starters.

## Common mistakes
- Searching one database with one phrase.
- Listing papers instead of synthesising.
- Treating every study as equal.
- Ignoring dates and settings.
- Not recording search and screening decisions.

## Output format
```
1. Question and criteria
2. Search log
3. Screening summary (numbers and exclusion reasons)
4. Extraction table
5. Quality assessment
6. Synthesis with confidence and gaps
```

## Self-check before you answer
- Were the question and inclusion criteria set before searching?
- Were several databases searched and logged?
- Was study quality assessed?
- Does the synthesis explain patterns, confidence and gaps?
