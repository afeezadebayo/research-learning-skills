# Active Reading and Notes

Most reading is wasted. People highlight, reread and close the book or article with little they can use a week later. A large review of learning techniques by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated highlighting and rereading as low-utility study methods, while practice testing and spaced practice rated high. [Research, Dunlosky et al. 2013] Active reading changes the goal from "getting through" a text to extracting what matters for a purpose, writing it in your own words and connecting it to what you already know, so the ideas can be found and used later.

This skill reads books, reports, papers and articles actively and turns them into useful notes: setting a purpose, previewing before reading, reading at the right depth, questioning the text, writing notes in your own words, capturing sources and quotes accurately, linking notes to projects and reviewing them so knowledge stays available.

## Operating principles
1. **Read with a purpose.** Know the question or use before you start.
2. **Preview first.** Structure, summary and conclusions guide where to spend time.
3. **Match depth to value.** Skim most, read some closely, study a few deeply.
4. **Write in your own words.** Paraphrasing forces understanding; copying does not.
5. **Capture sources precisely.** Page numbers and exact quotes when quoting.
6. **Connect and use.** Notes link to projects, questions and other notes.

## Reading depths
| Depth | What you do | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Skim (5 to 10 min) | Title, summary, headings, figures, conclusions | Deciding whether to read; getting the gist |
| Read (30 to 60 min) | Read key sections, note main arguments and evidence | Most work reading |
| Study (hours) | Read fully, question, summarise, test yourself | Core sources, exams, deep expertise |
Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren's How to Read a Book describes similar levels, from inspectional to analytical reading. [Reference, How to Read a Book]

## When to use, and when not to
Use it for books, research papers, reports, long articles, documentation and course material you need to understand, apply or cite.

Don't apply deep note-taking to everything; most content only needs a skim and a one-line note or nothing at all.

## Inputs
- The text and why you are reading it.
- Your note-taking tool (paper, notes app, Obsidian, Notion or similar).
- Projects or questions the reading supports.
- Time available.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Set the purpose
Write one or two questions you want the text to answer, or the decision it informs.

### Step 2. Preview
Read the title, summary or abstract, contents, headings, figures and conclusions. Decide the depth.

### Step 3. Read actively
Ask as you read: what is the main claim? What evidence supports it? Do I agree? How does it connect to what I know?

### Step 4. Pause and recall
At the end of each section, close the text and write the key point from memory. This retrieval strengthens memory more than rereading.

### Step 5. Write notes in your own words
One idea per note where possible, with the source and page.

### Step 6. Capture quotes carefully
Only quotes you may use, exactly as written, with page numbers.

### Step 7. Summarise
A short summary of the whole text: main argument, key evidence, your view and how you will use it.

### Step 8. Connect
Link notes to projects, related notes and questions; tag by topic.

### Step 9. Review and use
Revisit notes when working on related tasks; turn key ideas into flashcards if you need to remember them.

## Note templates
**Book or report summary**
```
Title, author, year | Read: <date> | Depth: <skim/read/study>
Why I read it: <question>
Main argument (3 sentences, my words):
Key ideas (with pages):
1. ...
Evidence and examples:
What I disagree with or question:
How I will use it: <projects, actions>
Related notes: <links>
```
**Single idea note**
```
Idea: <one sentence in my words>
Explanation: <2 to 4 sentences>
Source: <author, title, page>
Links: <related ideas, projects>
```

## Questions to ask while reading
- What problem is the author solving?
- What are the key terms, and how are they defined?
- What is the strongest evidence, and what is missing?
- What would someone who disagrees say?
- What can I do differently because of this?

## Reading a research paper in 20 minutes
1. Title and abstract: what question and what answer?
2. Figures and tables: what do the main results show?
3. Conclusion and limitations: what do the authors claim and admit?
4. Introduction: why does it matter, and what came before?
5. Methods: only if you need to judge quality or reuse the approach.
Write a five-line note: question, method, main finding, limitations, relevance to you.

## Choosing what to read
Keep a reading list linked to goals and projects, not to everything that looks interesting. Before starting a book, skim the contents and a chapter; drop books that are not useful. Finishing everything you start is not a virtue when time is limited.

## Notes systems
| Approach | Good for |
|---|---|
| Summary per source | Quick reference to what a book or report says |
| Idea notes linked together (often called a Zettelkasten) | Building connected knowledge over years |
| Project notes | Collecting what is needed for one deliverable |
Many people combine all three: a summary per source, idea notes for key concepts and links into active projects.

## Using AI while reading
Ask an AI assistant to explain a hard passage in plain words, generate questions to test your understanding or compare an author's argument with others. Write the final notes yourself, because explaining ideas in your own words is what builds understanding.

Review your notes monthly and delete what no longer helps.

Link each summary to at least one project.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Very long reports | Read the summary and the sections that answer your questions |
| Dense academic papers | Abstract, figures, conclusion first; then methods if needed |
| Reading for exams | Study depth plus self-testing and spaced review |
| Reading on screens | Use annotation tools; export highlights then rewrite as notes |
| AI summaries | Use as a preview; read key sections yourself before relying on them |
| Many sources on one topic | Synthesis notes that compare authors |

## Worked example
A growth manager read business books but remembered little. She started writing one question before each book, previewing chapters, writing a three-sentence summary after each chapter from memory and creating one idea note per useful concept, linked to current projects. A book on pricing produced eight idea notes, two of which shaped a pricing test the following month. Six months later her notes held over 200 linked ideas she could search and reuse in proposals and talks.

## Common mistakes
- Reading without a purpose.
- Highlighting and rereading instead of recalling.
- Copying text instead of paraphrasing.
- No page numbers for quotes.
- Notes that are never connected or used.

## Output format
```
1. Purpose and questions
2. Chosen reading depth
3. Notes (summary and idea notes)
4. Quotes with pages
5. Links to projects and other notes
6. Follow-up actions and review plan
```

## Self-check before you answer
- Is there a clear purpose for reading?
- Are notes in your own words with sources and pages?
- Did you recall key points from memory?
- Are notes linked to projects or questions?
